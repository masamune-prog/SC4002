  2. Weaknesses, in order of likely impact

  W1. Unseen words all become <unk>, and the <unk> vector is never trained

  word2id is built from every training token with no minimum count, so no
  training token ever maps to <unk>. Row 1 of the embedding (<unk>) never gets a
  gradient. At validation and test time, every unseen word becomes a random
  N(0, 0.1) vector the encoder has never seen.

  GloVe very likely has a vector for most of those unseen words: song titles,
  artist names, place names, uncommon verbs. The notebook discards them because
  the vocabulary is limited to training words. This removes the main advantage
  of pretrained word vectors, which is generalising to words not in the training
  data. It mostly hurts open-class slots such as song_name, place_name, person,
  event_name and business_name, which are exactly the types where span-level F1
  suffers.

  W2. No character or sub-word information

  The model only sees whole words through a lookup table. Tokens like 5am,
  10:30, 7th, o'clock, i'm or a misspelling are either an <unk> or a rarely seen
  vector. Of the 5,302 training words, 384 have no GloVe vector at all. Time,
  date and number-like slots are frequent in MASSIVE and are largely
  recognisable from their characters.

  W3. Slot tags are predicted independently; BIO rules are only applied at
  decoding

  The slot head applies a separate softmax at each word. The Viterbi step
  removes invalid sequences, but the model never learns which tags tend to
  follow which (for example, B-date → I-date, or O → B-time after "at"). It also
  never learns these patterns during training. Slot F1 is scored on exact
  spans, so one wrong boundary counts as both a false positive and a false
  negative. A CRF output layer is the standard fix and typically gains a point
  or two of F1 on slot-filling benchmarks.

  W4. A transformer trained from scratch on 11.5k short utterances lacks local
  bias

  Self-attention has no built-in preference for neighbouring words, and the
  learned position table (64 × 128) has to discover word order from limited
  data. Positions beyond about 15 are seen rarely, since most utterances are
  short. BIO tagging is mostly a local task. LSTM and convolutional layers have
  this local bias built in, which is why BiLSTM-CRF remains a strong
  non-pretrained baseline. The flat result for 1 versus 2 layers is consistent
  with this: the encoder isn't using extra depth.

  W5. Parameters are spent on the embedding table, not the encoder

  About 89% of the 1.79M parameters are in the embedding table, which is fully
  fine-tuned. The encoder has only 132k. The projection from 300 to 128
  dimensions is a bottleneck (32 dimensions per attention head), and only 9% of
  the 20M budget is used. Fine-tuning 1.59M embedding values on 11.5k sentences
  also pulls training words away from their GloVe positions. Unseen synonyms
  then stop lining up with them, which makes W1 worse.

  W6. Weak regularisation and an outdated optimiser setup

  - Adam with a constant learning rate: no warmup, no decay, no weight decay, no
    gradient clipping. Post-LayerNorm transformers (norm_first=False) are known
    to need warmup. A constant learning rate also explains the noisy validation
    curves, because the model never settles.
  - Dropout of 0.1 is too low given how fast validation loss rises. There is no
    word dropout and no label smoothing.

  W7. Intent pooling and the lack of interaction between the tasks

  - Mean pooling gives filler words ("please", "can you") the same weight as the
    words that decide the intent ("wake", "remind").
  - The two heads only share the encoder. The intent head ignores the predicted
    slots, and the slot head ignores the predicted intent. Intents and slot
    types depend heavily on each other: alarm_set never has a song_name, and
    play_music rarely has time. The model can produce outputs that contradict
    each other.
  - The scenario field (18 scenarios; each intent is scenario_action) is never
    used, even though it is part of the training split and gives a coarse
    grouping of the intents.

  W8. The tasks share one loss weighting and one stopping point

  The loss is CE_intent + CE_slot with equal weights. Intent is learned and
  overfitted by epoch 10, while slots need about 30 epochs. Through the shared
  encoder, the intent loss keeps fitting the training set while the slot head is
  still learning.

  W9. The evaluation can't tell which change actually helps

  There is one seed, no variance estimate and no breakdown of errors. With
  roughly ±1 point of noise per epoch, improvements under about 1 point cannot
  be separated from luck.

❯ u mentioned developing a plain BiLSTM-CRF with char-CNN and GloVe. we have
  developed different architechtures independently, like CNN, transformers, CRF
  and BiLSTM. would combining these models into an ensemble help in this case

⏺ Yes, it will usually help. How much depends on two things you can check before
  building anything: whether the models are similarly strong, and whether they
  get different examples wrong. Models with different architectures tend to make
  more varied mistakes than copies of one model trained with different seeds,
  which is good for an ensemble. A clearly weaker model can still pull the
  result down if every model gets an equal vote.

  When it helps and when it doesn't

  It helps when:
  - The models make different mistakes. A CNN mostly uses short local patterns,
    a BiLSTM uses word order, a transformer uses the whole sentence, and a CRF
    uses which tag follows which. Where one model fails, another may get it
    right.
  - There's room above your best single model. Check this on the dev set with
    the "oracle" score: the share of utterances where at least one model gets
    the intent right (or the span right). If the oracle is far above your best
    single model, an ensemble has room to gain. If it's barely higher, the
    models make the same mistakes and an ensemble won't help much.
  - It reduces noise. Your training curves move about ±1 point per epoch, and
    averaging several models smooths out the luck of which checkpoint you
    picked. That alone is a reliable gain.

  It doesn't help, or it hurts, when:
  - One model is much weaker than the rest. For example, if your "CRF" is the
    stage-1 CRF with no hidden layers (going by the commit history), it is
    probably several F1 points behind the neural models. With equal votes it
    drags the result down. Give it a small weight, or leave it out.
  - The models' confidence isn't comparable. Your transformer's validation loss
    rises from 1.0 to 1.6, which means it is overconfident. If you average
    probabilities, it will outvote better models simply because it is more
    certain. Fix this with temperature scaling, fitted per model on the dev set,
    before averaging.

  How to combine them (each task needs a different method)

  Task 1, intent. This is easy:
  - Average the calibrated softmax probabilities across the models, with a
    weight per model, then take the top intent.
  - Simple majority voting also works, but it ties often with 4 models.

  Task 2, slots. This is harder, because a span is only correct if every word in
  it is tagged correctly. There are two good options:

  Method: Average the per-word tag probabilities, then decode
  How: Average each model's tag probabilities at every word, then run your
  BIO-constrained Viterbi on the average.
  Notes: Needs the same tokenisation (whitespace split) and the same 111-tag set

  in every model. CRF models don't output per-word probabilities directly, so
  compute them with the forward-backward algorithm.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Method: Vote on spans
  How: Each model outputs its set of (slot type, span text) pairs. Keep a span
  if
  at least k models predicted it, then resolve overlapping spans.
  Notes: Works on any model's output, CRF or not. It matches how F1 is scored.
  Tune k on dev: a lower k finds more spans (higher recall), a higher k makes
  fewer mistakes (higher precision).

  I'd try both and keep whichever scores better on dev. Avoid choosing one
  model's whole tag sequence per sentence; that wastes most of what the ensemble
  offers.

  Different models for each task. Each task can use its own set of models and
  weights. For example, the transformer might help the intent ensemble but hurt
  the slot ensemble. Because the combined score is just the average of the two
  task scores, treat them as separate problems.

  Check the final pair. An intent chosen by one vote and slots chosen by another
  can contradict each other, such as alarm_set with a song_name slot. Apply the
  intent-to-slot-type compatibility mask from my earlier list (item 12) after
  combining.

  Combine whole models, or build them into one model?

  These are two separate ideas, and you can do both:
  1. One combined model: char-CNN + GloVe → BiLSTM → transformer layers → CRF.
     This combines the strengths of each architecture, and the parts are trained
     together.
  2. An ensemble: combines the outputs of separately trained models.

  Usually the strongest result comes from building the best single combined

❯ letting each task use the other's prediction means the intent also plays a
  part in predicting slot tag? why is this needed

⏺ Yes. "Intent informs slots" means the slot tagger receives the model's intent
  prediction as an extra input when it chooses each word's tag. The other
  direction, "slots inform intent", does the reverse. Here is why it's useful
  and when it isn't.

  Why the slot tagger benefits from knowing the intent

  1. The same words can belong to different slot types, and the intent decides
  which.

  ┌───────────────┬───────┬─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┐
  │   Utterance   │ Span  │  Correct slot type  │      What decides it      │
  ├───────────────┼───────┼─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ "play paris   │ paris │ song_name           │ intent play_music         │
  │ by ..."       │       │                     │                           │
  ├───────────────┼───────┼─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ "weather in   │ paris │ place_name          │ intent weather_query      │
  │ paris"        │       │                     │                           │
  ├───────────────┼───────┼─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ "remind me to │       │ the reminder's      │                           │
  │  call mom"    │ mom   │ content, not a      │ intent calendar_set       │
  │               │       │ contact             │                           │
  ├───────────────┼───────┼─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ "call mom"    │ mom   │ relation / person   │ intent email_querycontact │
  │               │       │                     │  / call intent            │
  └───────────────┴───────┴─────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┘

  The boundaries are the easy part here. The hard part is the type, and the
  intent largely determines it. Span F1 needs both type and text to match
  exactly. So a wrong type costs twice: one false positive (the wrong type) plus
  one false negative (the missed correct type).

  2. Each intent only uses a few slot types. There are 55 slot types, but a
  given intent typically uses only a handful of them. alarm_set uses time and
  date, never song_name or currency_name. Knowing the intent shrinks the slot
  head's real choice from 111 tags to perhaps 10–15. That matters for a model
  learning from only 11.5k sentences.

  3. The two outputs should agree with each other. Right now the system can
  output alarm_set with a song_name slot. The two heads never see each other's
  decisions, so nothing prevents this.

  Doesn't the shared encoder already do this?

  Only indirectly, and only weakly in your baseline:
  - Each word's slot prediction only sees that word's encoder output. The slot
    head is a linear layer applied to one word at a time. Any sentence-level
    information must already be in that word's encoder output, and your selected
    model has just one attention layer to put it there.
  - Only the intent head is trained to summarise the whole sentence. It is about
    85% accurate, so its output is a strong, compact summary that the slot head
    never gets to use.
  - There may not be enough data to learn it indirectly. With 60 intents and
    11.5k examples, the encoder may not learn to carry "this is a music request"
    into every word's representation. Feeding it in directly gives the slot
    head that information for free.

  Published joint models report gains from this: slot-gated models,
  Stack-Propagation (Qin et al., 2019) and Co-Interactive Transformer (Qin et
  al., 2021). Most of the improvement is in slot F1.

  The other direction: slots help intent

  Detected slots are evidence for the intent. A song_name or artist_name span
  strongly suggests play_music, and a time span suggests alarm or calendar
  intents over, say, weather_query. This helps with intents that use similar
  wording, which is where much of your remaining 15% intent error probably is.

  How it is usually done

  - Soft conditioning (recommended): take the intent probabilities and compute a
    weighted mix of learned intent vectors (one vector per intent). Add that
    mix to each word's encoder output before the slot layer or CRF. A gated
    version (the slot-gated model) learns how much to rely on it at each word.
  - Hard compatibility mask (no parameters): from the training split, record
    which slot types ever appear with each intent. At decoding, forbid slot
    types never seen with the predicted intent.
  - Joint decoding: decode the slots for each of the top 2–3 intents and keep
    the pair with the highest combined score.

  The main risk: a wrong intent leads to wrong slots

  About 15% of intents are wrong. If the slot head trusts a wrong intent, it may
  now get slots wrong that it would have tagged correctly before. Ways to
  reduce this:
  - Pass on the intent probabilities, not just the top intent. When the model is
    unsure (say 0.5 play_music, 0.4 play_radio), the slot head sees that
    uncertainty.
  - Don't train with the gold intent only. If training always uses the correct
    intent, the slot head learns to trust it fully and breaks on wrong
    predictions at test time. Train with the predicted (soft) intent, or mix
    gold and predicted intents.
  - Use joint decoding over the top few intents, so one confident mistake
    doesn't decide everything.
  - Apply the hard mask by scenario rather than by intent. Scenario is the
    coarser label. It is wrong less often than the intent, and it still rules
    out most impossible slot types.

  Is it actually needed for your model?

  It's not required, and you can check whether it would pay off. On the dev
  predictions you already have:
  1. Count slot errors where the span text is right but the type is wrong. These
     are the errors that knowing the intent fixes.
  2. Count predicted slot types that never appear with the gold intent in the
     training split. These are the errors the hard mask would remove for free.

  If those two make up a large share of slot errors, this change is worth doing.
  If most errors are missed spans or wrong boundaries, the CRF and the
  character features from the earlier list will give more.
